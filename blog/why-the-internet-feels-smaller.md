The internet used to feel infinite. You could wander into a forum about
obscure Finnish architecture, end up reading someone’s personal diary
written in HTML, and stumble sideways into a community of people who
collected vintage train timetables.

That doesn’t happen anymore. Not really.

## The Funnel Problem

Every major platform now operates as a funnel. You enter at the top —
a search query, a notification, a link in a group chat — and you are
guided, gently but firmly, toward content optimised for your engagement.

The problem isn’t that the content is bad. The problem is that the
range has collapsed.

- Algorithmic curation replaces serendipity
- Engagement metrics reward the familiar over the strange
- Centralisation means fewer doors to knock on

## What We Lost

There was a specific texture to early internet culture: the sense that
you were always one click away from something completely unexpected.
A personal page. A half-finished project. An argument from 2003 that
no one had resolved.

That texture came from fragmentation. From the fact that no single
entity controlled the shape of the space.

## Reclaiming the Weird

Some of this is still out there. The small web exists. People still
run personal sites, gemini capsules, tilde communities.

The question is whether enough people will choose to look.
